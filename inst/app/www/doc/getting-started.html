What is Power Analysis?

The Core Question

Power analysis helps you answer one of three fundamental questions:

Question 1: Sample Size > “How many participants do I need to detect a meaningful effect?”

Question 2: Statistical Power > “Given my available sample size, what is my chance of detecting the effect?”

Question 3: Minimal Detectable Effect > “Given my sample size and desired power, what is the smallest effect I can reliably detect?”

Why Power Analysis Matters

Underpowered studies (too few participants): - Miss real effects (Type II error) - Waste resources and participant time - Contribute to publication bias - May be unethical if participants are exposed to risk

Overpowered studies (too many participants): - Waste resources on unnecessary recruitment - Expose more participants to potential risks - Detect statistically significant but clinically trivial effects

Well-powered studies (just right): - Balance efficiency with adequate detection capability - Maximize scientific and ethical value - Meet regulatory and funding requirements

The Four Key Components

Every power calculation involves exactly four components. If you know any three, you can calculate the fourth.

1. Sample Size (N)

Definition: The number of participants (or events) in your study.

Typical questions: - How many patients do I need to recruit? - How many matched pairs for a case-control study? - How many events for a survival analysis?

Typical values: Varies widely by design and effect size (from 50 to 5,000+)

2. Effect Size

Definition: The magnitude of the difference or association you want to detect.

Effect size measures (depends on outcome type):

Binary outcomes (Yes/No, Event/No Event): - Risk Difference (RD): Absolute difference in proportions (e.g., 10% vs 20% = 10 percentage points) - Relative Risk (RR): Ratio of proportions (e.g., 0.20/0.10 = 2.0) - Odds Ratio (OR): For case-control studies (e.g., OR = 2.5) - Hazard Ratio (HR): For time-to-event outcomes (e.g., HR = 0.75)

Continuous outcomes (e.g., blood pressure, BMI): - Cohen’s d: Standardized mean difference - Small: d = 0.2 - Medium: d = 0.5 - Large: d = 0.8 - Raw difference: Actual units (e.g., 5 mmHg blood pressure reduction)

How to choose an effect size:

  1. Clinical importance: What difference would change clinical practice?
  2. Prior literature: What effects have similar studies found?
  3. Pilot data: What preliminary estimates do you have?
  4. Regulatory standards: What margins are accepted by FDA/EMA?

3. Statistical Power (1 - β)

Definition: The probability of detecting an effect if it truly exists.

Common convention: Power ≥ 0.80 (80%)

Interpretation: - Power = 0.80 means you have an 80% chance of finding a significant result if the effect is real - Power = 0.50 means you’re essentially flipping a coin (inadequate) - Power = 0.90 is higher confidence but requires more participants

When to use higher power: - Costly interventions - Irreversible outcomes - Regulatory submissions (often require 90% power) - Confirmatory trials

4. Significance Level (α)

Definition: The probability of a false positive (Type I error rate).

Common convention: α = 0.05 (5%)

One-sided vs. two-sided: - Two-sided (default): Tests whether groups differ in either direction (e.g., “Is A different from B?”) - One-sided: Tests whether one group is higher/lower (e.g., “Is A better than B?”) - Use for non-inferiority or superiority trials - Requires strong prior justification - α = 0.025 for one-sided test (equivalent to two-sided α = 0.05)

Choosing the Right Analysis Type

The tool supports 9 different analysis types. Here’s how to choose:

Decision Tree

Step 1: What type of outcome do you have?

Binary outcome (Yes/No, Event/No Event, Success/Failure): - → Go to Step 2

Continuous outcome (measured on a scale: weight, blood pressure, QoL score): - → Continuous Outcomes (t-test)

Time-to-event outcome (survival, time to hospitalization, time to cure): - → Go to Step 3

Step 2: Binary Outcome - How many groups?

One group (comparing to a known rate): - → Single Proportion (Rule of 3) - Example: “What is the infection rate in our hospital compared to the national average?”

Two groups (comparing two treatments/exposures): - Are cases and controls matched (paired)? - Yes → Matched Case-Control Study - No → Two-Group Comparison (Proportions)

Step 3: Time-to-Event - What type of comparison?

Testing for a difference: - → Survival Analysis (Cox Regression)

Testing for equivalence or non-inferiority: - → Survival Equivalence/Non-Inferiority

Step 4: Special Study Designs

Non-inferiority or equivalence trial (binary outcome): - → Non-Inferiority Testing

Mediation analysis (testing indirect effects): - → Mediation Analysis Power

Observational study with propensity score weighting: - → Propensity Score Sample Size

Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Simple Two-Group Comparison

Research Question: Does a new smoking cessation intervention improve quit rates compared to standard care?

Study Design: - Two groups: Intervention vs. Standard Care - Outcome: Binary (Quit smoking: Yes/No) - Equal allocation (1:1 ratio)

Known information: - Standard care quit rate: 20% (from literature) - Intervention quit rate: 35% (clinically meaningful improvement) - Desired power: 80% - Significance level: α = 0.05 (two-sided)

Step 1: Navigate to “Two-Group Comparison” tab in the tool

Step 2: Select “Calculate Sample Size”

Step 3: Enter parameters: - Group 1 proportion: 35 (intervention) - Group 2 proportion: 20 (standard care) - Desired power: 0.80 - Significance level: 0.05 - Test type: Two-sided - Allocation ratio: 1.0 (equal groups)

Step 4: Click “Calculate”

Expected Result:

Sample size per group: 150 participants
Total sample size: 300 participants

Effect Measures:
- Risk Difference (RD): 15 percentage points
- Relative Risk (RR): 1.75
- Odds Ratio (OR): 2.17

Interpretation: You need to recruit 150 participants per group (300 total) to have an 80% chance of detecting a 15 percentage point improvement in quit rates.

Example 2: Feasibility-Constrained Study (Minimal Detectable Effect)

Research Question: What effect size can we detect with our limited budget?

Study Design: - Two groups comparing continuous outcome (systolic blood pressure) - Maximum affordable sample: N = 100 (50 per group)

Known information: - Available sample: 50 per group - Desired power: 80% - Significance level: α = 0.05

Step 1: Navigate to “Continuous Outcomes” tab

Step 2: Select “Calculate Minimal Detectable Effect”

Step 3: Enter parameters: - Sample size per group: 50 - Desired power: 0.80 - Significance level: 0.05 - Test type: Two-sided

Step 4: Click “Calculate”

Expected Result:

Minimal Detectable Effect Size (Cohen's d): 0.57 (medium effect)

With n=50 per group, you can reliably detect:
- Effects of medium size or larger (d ≥ 0.57)
- You are underpowered for small effects (d < 0.5)

Interpretation: With 50 participants per group, you can detect medium-to-large effects but will miss small effects. Decide whether this is acceptable based on your research question.

Example 3: Survival Analysis (Time-to-Event)

Research Question: Does a new medication reduce time to hospital readmission?

Study Design: - Cox proportional hazards analysis - Outcome: Time to readmission (days) - Comparing treated vs. untreated patients

Known information: - Expected hazard ratio: HR = 0.70 (30% reduction in hazard) - Proportion exposed (treated): 50% - Overall event rate: 40% (40% of patients readmitted during follow-up) - Desired power: 80% - Significance level: α = 0.05

Step 1: Navigate to “Survival Analysis” tab

Step 2: Select “Calculate Sample Size”

Step 3: Enter parameters: - Hazard ratio: 0.70 - Proportion exposed: 0.50 - Event rate: 40% - Desired power: 0.80 - Significance level: 0.05

Step 4: Click “Calculate”

Expected Result:

Total sample size required: 426 participants
Number of events required: 170 events

With HR = 0.70:
- Study needs to observe 170 readmission events
- Requires enrolling 426 participants (given 40% event rate)

Interpretation: Unlike sample-size based analyses, survival studies are event-driven. You need 170 events (readmissions), which requires following 426 participants until 40% experience the event.

Understanding Adjustments and Special Features

Missing Data Adjustment

Why it matters: Not all enrolled participants provide complete data.

Example scenario: - Calculated sample size: 300 participants - Expected dropout rate: 20% - Missing data mechanism: MAR (Missing At Random) - Analysis plan: Complete-case analysis

Adjustment:

Adjusted sample size: 375 participants
- Base calculation: 300
- Inflation factor: 1.25 (to account for 20% missing data)
- Additional participants: 75

Tool feature: Check “Adjust for missing data” and specify your dropout rate and analysis plan (complete-case vs. multiple imputation).

Clustering Adjustment (Design Effect)

Why it matters: When participants are clustered (e.g., patients within hospitals), observations are not independent.

Example scenario: - Calculated sample size (assuming independence): 200 participants - Study design: Patients nested within 10 hospitals - Average cluster size: 20 patients per hospital - Intraclass Correlation (ICC): 0.05

Adjustment:

Design Effect (DE) = 1 + (m-1) × ICC
                   = 1 + (20-1) × 0.05
                   = 1.95

Adjusted sample size = 200 × 1.95 = 390 participants

Tool feature: Check “Adjust for clustering” and specify ICC and cluster size.

Multiple Testing Correction

Why it matters: Testing multiple hypotheses increases false positive risk.

Example scenario: - Primary outcome + 2 secondary outcomes = 3 tests - Nominal α = 0.05 per test - Correction method: Bonferroni

Adjustment:

Adjusted α = 0.05 / 3 = 0.0167 per test

Sample size increase: ~15-20% to maintain power

Tool feature: Check “Adjust for multiple testing” and specify number of comparisons and correction method.

Interpreting and Reporting Results

What to Report in Your Protocol

When presenting power analysis results, include:

  1. Study design: (e.g., “Two-group parallel randomized trial”)
  2. Primary outcome: (e.g., “Binary outcome: 30-day mortality”)
  3. Effect size assumption: (e.g., “Reduction from 20% to 15% (RD=5%, RR=0.75)”)
  4. Justification: (e.g., “Based on Smith et al. (2020) pilot study”)
  5. Sample size: (e.g., “150 per group, 300 total”)
  6. Power: (e.g., “80% power at α=0.05 (two-sided)”)
  7. Adjustments: (e.g., “Inflated by 20% for expected dropout (final N=375)”)

Example paragraph (generated by the tool):

A sample size of 150 participants per group (300 total) provides 80% power to detect a risk difference of 5 percentage points (from 20% to 15%), corresponding to a relative risk of 0.75, using a two-sided test at the 0.05 significance level. This effect size is based on findings from Smith et al. (2020) and represents a clinically meaningful reduction in 30-day mortality. The sample size is inflated by 20% (final N=375) to account for expected dropout.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Pitfall 1: Post-hoc power analysis - DON’T: Calculate power after seeing non-significant results - WHY: Power is for planning, not post-hoc rationalization - DO INSTEAD: Report confidence intervals and effect estimates

Pitfall 2: Overly optimistic effect sizes - DON’T: Assume large effects without justification - WHY: Leads to underpowered studies that fail - DO INSTEAD: Use conservative estimates from literature or pilot data

Pitfall 3: Ignoring multiple comparisons - DON’T: Plan 10 secondary analyses without correction - WHY: Inflates false positive rate - DO INSTEAD: Pre-specify primary outcome and adjust α or use correction

Pitfall 4: Forgetting about feasibility - DON’T: Calculate N=5,000 when you can only recruit 500 - WHY: Sets up study for failure - DO INSTEAD: Calculate minimal detectable effect for N=500 and assess feasibility
