Missing Data Adjustment Strategies for Sample Size Calculations

Power Analysis Tool Development Team

2025-10-26

Introduction

Missing data is ubiquitous in real-world studies. Failing to account for missingness during sample size planning can lead to underpowered studies that cannot detect true effects. This vignette explains:

  1. Why missing data reduces power and effective sample size
  2. Three missing data mechanisms (MCAR, MAR, MNAR) and their implications
  3. Two analysis approaches: Complete Case Analysis (CCA) vs Multiple Imputation (MI)
  4. How to calculate sample size inflation for each approach
  5. Practical decision-making for choosing between CCA and MI

Why Missing Data Matters for Power

Statistical power depends on effective sample size — the number of observations actually used in analysis. Missing data reduces this:

  • Lost observations: Each missing case reduces the dataset
  • Reduced precision: Smaller sample → wider confidence intervals → lower power
  • Increased variance: Uncertainty increases with less complete data

Key principle: Always plan for more participants than theoretically required to account for expected missingness.

Missing Data Mechanisms

Understanding the mechanism of missingness is critical for choosing the right approach and interpreting results.

MCAR: Missing Completely at Random

Definition: Missingness is unrelated to any observed or unobserved variables.

Example: A lab machine randomly malfunctions, causing 10% of blood samples to be lost.

Properties: - Complete cases are a random sample of the full data - No bias introduced (only loss of power) - CCA is valid but inefficient - MI offers efficiency gains

Analogy: Randomly flipping a coin to decide whether to measure someone — the flip has nothing to do with their characteristics or outcomes.

MAR: Missing at Random

Definition: Missingness depends on observed variables, but not on the missing values themselves (conditional on observed data).

Example: Older patients are more likely to miss follow-up visits, but among patients of the same age, missingness is random. If we know age, we can account for the missingness pattern.

Properties: - Complete cases may be biased (e.g., overrepresenting younger patients) - CCA can introduce bias unless the analysis model includes predictors of missingness - MI can provide unbiased estimates if the imputation model includes predictors of missingness - Most common mechanism in practice

Analogy: Whether a student submits homework depends on their GPA (observed), but conditional on GPA, missingness is random.

MNAR: Missing Not at Random

Definition: Missingness depends on unobserved values (the missing data itself).

Example: Patients with severe side effects are more likely to drop out, but we don’t observe the severity for those who dropped out.

Properties: - Both CCA and MI can be biased - Bias cannot be fully eliminated without additional information - Sensitivity analysis required to assess impact - May require specialized methods (e.g., selection models, pattern mixture models)

Analogy: Students who would have failed the exam are more likely not to show up (the missing exam score is related to what it would have been).

Practical Guidance

In most studies:

  1. Assume MAR unless you have strong reason to suspect MNAR
  2. Plan for sensitivity analyses to explore MNAR scenarios
  3. Collect predictors of missingness during study design (e.g., age, baseline severity) to strengthen MAR assumption and improve MI models

Two Analysis Approaches

Complete Case Analysis (CCA)

Method: Use only observations with complete data; discard any observation with missingness.

Advantages: - Simple and transparent - No modeling assumptions about missing data - Unbiased under MCAR - Unbiased under MAR if missingness predictors are in the analysis model

Disadvantages: - Inefficient: discards partial information - Requires larger sample sizes - Can introduce bias under MAR if not handled carefully - Substantial power loss with high missingness

Sample size adjustment:

\[ N_{CCA} = \frac{N_{required}}{1 - p_{missing}} \]

where \(p_{missing}\) is the proportion of expected missingness.

Example: If 20% missingness expected and N = 400 required, need N = 400 / 0.80 = 500 to ensure 400 complete cases.

Multiple Imputation (MI)

Method: Create multiple plausible datasets by imputing missing values based on observed data, analyze each dataset separately, then pool results using Rubin’s rules.

Advantages: - More efficient: uses all available information - Reduces sample size inflation compared to CCA - Unbiased under MAR with good imputation model - Can incorporate auxiliary variables not in analysis model

Disadvantages: - More complex to implement - Requires careful imputation model specification - Can be biased if imputation model is misspecified - Requires sufficient computational resources for many imputations

Sample size adjustment:

\[ N_{MI} = N_{required} \times \frac{1}{1 - p_{missing}} \times \sqrt{\frac{1}{RE}} \]

where RE is the relative efficiency of MI, which depends on: - Number of imputations (m) - Imputation model quality (R²) - Fraction of missing information (FMI)

Key insight: MI recovers information lost to missingness, so requires fewer additional participants than CCA.

Observations:

  1. Higher missingness → greater MI advantage: At 40% missingness, MI saves 115 participants (17.2%)
  2. MI is always more efficient than CCA when R² > 0
  3. Diminishing returns: Even excellent imputation models cannot fully eliminate inflation at high missingness

Observations:

  1. R² matters: Excellent models (R² = 0.80) save 54 participants vs CCA; poor models (R² = 0.20) save only 17
  2. Even poor imputation is better than CCA (unless grossly misspecified)
  3. Invest in good predictors: Including strong auxiliary variables improves R² and efficiency

Effect of Number of Imputations (m)

With 20% missingness and R² = 0.60:

MI Efficiency by Number of Imputations (20% Missingness, R² = 0.60)
Number_of_Imputations m N_MI Relative_Efficiency Marginal_Gain
m = 5 5 466 0.981 —
m = 10 10 460 0.986 6

m = 100 100 456 0.991 1

Observations:

  1. Diminishing returns: Going from m = 5 to m = 10 saves 6 participants; m = 50 to m = 100 saves only 1
  2. Rule of thumb: Use m ≥ % missingness (e.g., 20% missing → m ≥ 20)
  3. Practical recommendation: m = 20 to 50 is sufficient for most applications
  4. Computational cost: Higher m increases analysis time; balance efficiency gains vs runtime

Practical Decision Framework

Step 1: Estimate Expected Missingness

From pilot data, literature, or expert judgment:

  • Low: < 10%
  • Moderate: 10-25%
  • High: > 25%

Sources: - Similar studies in same population - Pilot study retention rates - Domain expertise (e.g., longitudinal studies typically have higher missingness)

Step 2: Identify Mechanism

Ask: - Is missingness related to any observed variables? (If yes → likely MAR) - Could missingness depend on unobserved values? (If yes → potentially MNAR; plan sensitivity analysis)

Default assumption: MAR (most common in practice)

Step 3: Choose Analysis Approach

Choose CCA if:

  • Very low missingness (< 5%): Efficiency difference negligible
  • Simple study design with no auxiliary variables
  • Reviewers/stakeholders prefer simpler methods
  • Imputation model difficult to specify (e.g., complex interactions, non-normal data)

Choose MI if:

  • Moderate to high missingness (≥ 10%): MI offers substantial efficiency gains
  • Good auxiliary variables available to predict missingness
  • MAR assumption plausible
  • Resources available for proper MI implementation

Step 4: Calculate Inflation Factor

For CCA:

result <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = [your required N],
  missing_pct = [expected % missing],
  mechanism = "mar",  # or "mcar" or "mnar"
  analysis_type = "complete_case"
)

For MI:

result <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = [your required N],
  missing_pct = [expected % missing],
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 20,       # Adjust based on % missing
  mi_r_squared = 0.60        # Estimate from pilot or literature
)

Step 5: Plan for Worst-Case Scenarios

Conduct sensitivity analysis:

# Worst case: higher missingness, poorer imputation model
worst_case <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 400,
  missing_pct = 30,          # Increase from expected 20%
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.40        # Decrease from expected 0.60
)

# Best case: lower missingness, better imputation
best_case <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 400,
  missing_pct = 10,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 20,
  mi_r_squared = 0.75
)

cat("Worst case N:", worst_case$n_inflated, "\n")
cat("Best case N:", best_case$n_inflated, "\n")

Use the worst-case estimate for conservative planning.

Case Study: Longitudinal RCT

Scenario

A 12-month randomized trial comparing a behavioral intervention to usual care for weight loss.

Parameters: - Primary outcome: Weight change at 12 months - Required sample size (complete data): N = 300 (150 per arm) - Expected missingness: 25% (based on similar studies) - Mechanism: MAR (dropout related to baseline BMI and early weight change, which will be measured) - Available auxiliary variables: Baseline BMI, demographics, 3-month and 6-month weight - Imputation model R² estimate: 0.65 (good predictors available)

CCA Approach

result_cca <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 300,
  missing_pct = 25,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "complete_case"
)

# Result: N = 400 (add 100 participants)

Interpretation: Recruit 400 participants (200 per arm) to ensure 300 complete cases.

MI Approach

result_mi <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 300,
  missing_pct = 25,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.65
)

# Result: N = 343 (add 43 participants)
# Efficiency gain: 57 fewer than CCA (14.3% savings)

Interpretation: Recruit 343 participants (172 per arm) using MI, saving 57 participants compared to CCA.

Budget Analysis

  • Cost per participant: $500 (recruitment, intervention, measurements)
  • CCA cost: 400 × $500 = $200,000
  • MI cost: 343 × $500 = $171,500
  • Savings with MI: $28,500 (14.3%)

Decision: Use MI approach given substantial cost savings and availability of good auxiliary predictors.

Sensitivity Analysis

Test robustness to assumptions:

# Scenario 1: Higher missingness (30% instead of 25%)
sens_high_miss <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 300,
  missing_pct = 30,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.65
)
# Result: N = 368 (vs 343 baseline)

# Scenario 2: Poorer imputation model (R² = 0.45 instead of 0.65)
sens_poor_model <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 300,
  missing_pct = 25,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.45
)
# Result: N = 356 (vs 343 baseline)

# Scenario 3: Both (worst case)
sens_worst <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 300,
  missing_pct = 30,
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.45
)
# Result: N = 382 (still better than CCA's 400)

Conclusion: Even under worst-case assumptions (30% missingness, R² = 0.45), MI requires N = 382, still 18 fewer participants than CCA’s 400.

Final decision: Recruit N = 380 (rounded up from worst-case 382) to provide adequate power across plausible scenarios.

Advanced Topics

Handling MNAR Missingness

If MNAR is suspected:

  1. Conduct CCA as primary analysis (conservative, avoids imputation model assumptions)
  2. Perform MI as sensitivity analysis (with different imputation models)
  3. Explore pattern mixture models or selection models if feasible
  4. Report results under multiple scenarios to assess robustness
result_mnar_cca <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 400,
  missing_pct = 20,
  mechanism = "mnar",
  analysis_type = "complete_case"
)

result_mnar_mi <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 400,
  missing_pct = 20,
  mechanism = "mnar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 25,
  mi_r_squared = 0.55
)

Differential Missingness by Treatment Arm

If missingness differs between treatment groups:

  1. Use the higher missingness rate for conservative inflation
  2. Account for unequal retention in primary analysis
  3. Report missingness by arm (important for interpretation)
# Treatment arm: 15% missingness
# Control arm: 30% missingness
# Use 30% for both arms to be conservative

result_conservative <- calc_missing_data_inflation(
  n_required = 400,
  missing_pct = 30,  # Higher of the two rates
  mechanism = "mar",
  analysis_type = "multiple_imputation",
  mi_imputations = 30,
  mi_r_squared = 0.60
)

Choosing Number of Imputations

Rule of thumb: m ≥ % missingness

  • 10% missing → m ≥ 10
  • 25% missing → m ≥ 25
  • 40% missing → m ≥ 40

Practical guidelines: - Minimum: m = 5 (rarely adequate; avoid) - Standard: m = 20-30 (suitable for most studies) - High missingness: m = 50-100 (if > 30% missing)

Computational note: Modern computers can handle m = 50-100 easily; prioritize statistical validity over runtime.

Summary

Key takeaways:

  1. Always account for missing data during sample size planning
  2. CCA requires larger samples than MI (simple formula: N / (1 - p_missing))
  3. MI offers efficiency gains of 5-20% depending on missingness rate and imputation model quality
  4. Assume MAR in most cases; plan sensitivity analyses for MNAR if suspected
  5. Invest in auxiliary variables to improve imputation model R² and efficiency
  6. Use m ≥ % missingness for number of imputations
  7. Conduct sensitivity analysis to assess robustness to assumptions

Recommended workflow:

  1. Estimate expected missingness from pilot data or literature
  2. Identify likely mechanism (default: MAR)
  3. Choose analysis approach (MI preferred for ≥ 10% missingness)
  4. Calculate inflation factor using appropriate method
  5. Plan for worst-case scenarios (higher missingness, poorer imputation)
  6. Document assumptions in protocol and report missingness patterns in results
